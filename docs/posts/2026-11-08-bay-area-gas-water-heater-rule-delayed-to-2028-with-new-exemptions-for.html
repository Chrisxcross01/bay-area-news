Bay Area Gas Water Heater Rule Delayed to 2028, With New Exemptions for Homeowners

Sun Nov 08 2026

Bay Area homeowners now have an extra year before most replacement water heaters must be electric. On November 4, 2026, the Bay Area Air District's Board of Directors voted to amend Rule 9-6, its water heater rule. According to the Air District, the change moves the zero-NOx (zero nitrogen oxides) standard from January 2027 to January 2028. The board also approved several new exemptions. Opponents in the South Bay say they will keep pushing for the rule to be repealed, so this probably isn't the last we'll hear of it.

What changed

The Air District first set a zero-NOx standard for residential water heaters when it amended Rule 9-6 in 2023. In practice, the standard rules out new gas-fired models, and the alternative that meets it is a heat pump water heater. According to the Air District's announcement, the amendments adopted this month:

  • Move the compliance date from January 2027 to January 2028.
  • Apply only at replacement. No one has to remove a working water heater. The rule does not cover stoves, dryers or fireplaces. KQED reported that repairing an existing gas unit is still allowed.
  • Extend the deadline to January 1, 2033 for water heaters in multifamily buildings and deed-restricted affordable housing, and for some specialized and large units. KQED described the multifamily extension as covering buildings with more than five units.
  • Exclude some units entirely. Water heaters of 30 gallons or less and mobile home water heaters don't have to meet the standard.
  • Allow temporary installations in emergencies.

Who may be exempt

The Air District lists three main exemption categories:

  1. Low-income property owners. This means owners earning less than 80% of Area Median Income, including people enrolled in programs such as Medi-Cal or CalFresh. It also covers households that spend more than 28% of their income on their mortgage and property taxes. In Santa Clara County, San José Spotlight reports that 80% of median income is $113,700 a year for a one-person household.
  2. Space-constrained properties that don't have enough room or ventilation for a compliant unit.
  3. Properties with electrical limits. Examples include panels under 100 amps, panels without enough spare capacity, or homes where a new electrical line would have to run more than 50 feet.

KQED reported that owners would claim the income exemption by self-reporting through an online portal. The electrical and space exemptions are one-time exemptions, and a contractor would have to document the electrical constraint. Air District officials estimate that about half of Bay Area water heaters will fall under an exemption, according to San José Spotlight.

Supporters and opponents

San José Spotlight reports that South Bay leaders are split. San Jose District 4 Councilmember David Cohen organized a rally in support of the rule. He told the outlet that the delay gives the public more time to adjust. Johnny Khamis is president of the Silicon Valley Business Alliance and a former San Jose councilmember. He argued that the board should have repealed the rule and replaced it with an incentive program. He also said he doubts the Air District will manage the exemptions well. Kat Snyder of 350 Silicon Valley told the outlet she was surprised by how many exemptions there are, but she accepted the compromise overall.

KQED reported that hundreds of people commented before the vote. Environmental, medical, faith and youth groups mostly supported the rule, and homeowner groups mostly opposed it because of cost. A few board members voted no or left the meeting in protest. The Air District says pollution from gas water heaters and furnaces causes up to 85 premature deaths and 15,000 asthma attacks in the Bay Area each year. A separate rule aimed at phasing out new gas furnaces is scheduled for 2029, according to KQED and San José Spotlight.

What it costs, and what help is available

The Air District estimates that a heat pump water heater costs about $3,500 more to buy and install than a gas model. Local rebates can cover some of that:

  • San José Clean Energy offers up to $3,500 for income-qualified residents, according to San José Spotlight. Its EcoHome Rebate rules say you must apply and have the funds reserved before any work starts. The old gas appliance has to be removed and the gas line capped, and you must show proof of a permit.
  • Silicon Valley Clean Energy, which serves other Santa Clara County cities such as Mountain View, lists its own heat pump water heater rebates, with higher amounts for income-qualified customers.

Rebate amounts and funding change often, so check the program websites for the current terms before you schedule an installation.

What this means for homeowners, buyers and sellers

  • Homeowners: If your gas water heater is getting old, use the extra year to plan. Find out how old the unit is, and ask an electrician whether your panel can take a heat pump. That way you won't be choosing in a rush after a leak. If you think you qualify for an exemption, read the Air District's guidance first.
  • Buyers: The home inspection should note the water heater's age and fuel type and the size of the electrical panel. A gas unit near the end of its life may need replacing after 2027, and that should be part of your budget. Talk with your lender if you'd like to finance upgrades.
  • Sellers: You don't have to replace a working gas water heater before you sell. Still, buyers may ask about it, so having permit records and the unit's age on hand can help.
  • Small landlords: Whether you get the 2033 date depends on the building type. Read the rule text or ask a qualified professional how it applies to your property.

The Air District's Building Appliances page covers the timeline, exemptions and rebates. If the income- or mortgage-based exemption could affect your taxes or finances, a tax professional can help.
