California's 2027 ADU Laws: Two Detached Units Per Lot and Lower Impact Fees

Fri Nov 27 2026

If you own a single-family home in San Jose, Santa Clara, Sunnyvale, Campbell or elsewhere in the Bay Area, your options for building backyard homes will widen in about a month. Governor Gavin Newsom signed two accessory dwelling unit (ADU) bills on September 29, 2026: Assembly Bill 956 and Senate Bill 1117. According to the Legislature's records and Wisevoter's coverage, both laws take effect January 1, 2027.

Below we explain what each law changes, who it affects and what you may want to do now.

AB 956: Two detached ADUs instead of one

AB 956, by Assemblymember Sharon Quirk-Silva, became Chapter 791 of the Statutes of 2026. Under current state law, a city or county has to approve one detached, newly built ADU on a lot with an existing or proposed single-family home. The approval is "ministerial," which means it follows set standards and doesn't go through a discretionary hearing. The Legislative Counsel's Digest says AB 956 raises that number to two.

Here are the main points, based on the bill records and Wisevoter's summary:

  • Setbacks: To get this guaranteed approval, the units can't be required to have side and rear setbacks of more than 4 feet.
  • Size and height: ADU industry summaries of the law say cities may limit each of these detached units to 800 square feet of living space and apply the state's height limits. Check your own city's standards before you design anything.
  • Junior ADUs: Under current law, a lot can have a conversion ADU, a detached ADU and a junior ADU (JADU). Once a lot has two new detached ADUs, the city no longer has to approve a JADU there.
  • HOAs and deed restrictions: AB 956 amends Civil Code Sections 714.3 and 4751. According to Wisevoter, this extends the state's limits on HOA and covenant rules that block ADUs to lots zoned for single-family residential use.

SB 1117: Impact fees only on space above 750 square feet

SB 1117, by Senator Sabrina Cervantes, became Chapter 809 of the Statutes of 2026. Under current law, cities, special districts and water companies can't charge impact fees on an ADU with 750 square feet or less of interior living space. Once a unit goes over 750 square feet, fees are charged in proportion to the size of the main house, and that calculation covers the whole ADU.

According to the bill text, SB 1117 keeps the proportional formula for up to two ADUs on a lot, but fees will apply only to the area above 750 square feet. For example, a 900-square-foot unit would be charged on its extra 150 square feet, not on all 900. A legal analysis by the law firm Best Best & Krieger, republished by PublicCEO, says the first 750 square feet of each unit is excluded from the calculation. Wisevoter reports that the law declares this a matter of statewide concern, so it applies in charter cities too.

Other charges, such as utility connection charges, may follow different rules. Ask your city and your water and sewer providers for a fee estimate.

What this means for Bay Area owners, buyers and sellers

These are statewide rules, so they apply in every Bay Area city and county. Many local governments have their own ADU ordinances, though, and those may need updating to match. Here are some practical steps:

  1. Check with your city's planning department. Before you hire a designer, ask how your city will handle two-detached-ADU applications starting January 1. Ask whether your project would be reviewed under the old rules or the new ones, depending on when you submit.
  2. Measure your lot carefully. On deeper or wider lots, the 4-foot setback limit may leave room for two small detached units. Site plans, utility locations, trees and easements will still decide what actually fits.
  3. Reconsider unit size. Under SB 1117, going a little over 750 square feet won't trigger fees on the whole unit. That may change the size you choose.
  4. Review HOA documents. If you live in a planned development or a single-family neighborhood with CC&Rs, ask an attorney or your HOA how AB 956 affects any ADU restrictions.
  5. Plan financing and taxes. Talk with a lender about how to pay for construction. Ask a tax professional how new construction could affect your property tax bill and income taxes, especially if you plan to rent the units.

Buyers looking at single-family homes with larger yards may want to factor in what the property could hold in the future. Still, confirm with the city before you count on it. Sellers should be careful about advertising ADU potential as guaranteed, because utilities, easements, HOA rules and site conditions vary from lot to lot. Small landlords thinking about rental units should also check local rental rules, which are separate from these ADU laws.

California has changed its ADU laws in almost every recent legislative session, and how these changes play out will depend partly on how each city puts them into practice. We'll keep watching how San Jose and nearby cities respond after January 1.
